The Ising model: the key results

Put a tiny magnet on every square of a grid. Each one only cares about its four neighbours, yet below one precise temperature, $T_c = 2/\ln(1+\sqrt2)\approx 2.269$, billions of them agree to point the same way. This page lets you run the model yourself, and see why that temperature is so strange.

1The lattice, live

Each square is a spin, up (gold) or down (blue). Neighbours that agree lower the energy; a field $h$ favours one colour: $$H = -\sum_{\langle ij\rangle} s_i s_j \;-\; h\sum_i s_i,$$ $$P(\text{configuration}) \propto e^{-H/T}.$$ The Metropolis rule picks a spin and flips it with probability $\min(1, e^{-\Delta E/T})$: downhill always, uphill sometimes, more often when hot. Cool the lattice below $T_c$ and watch domains grow; heat it and it melts into noise. Draw an island with the mouse (or your finger) and see whether it shrinks or survives.

Speed is in sweeps per frame (one sweep = one update attempt per spin). Near $T_c$ Metropolis struggles: big domains take forever to turn over (critical slowing down). Wolff flips whole clusters at once and does not care. Spins are updated in a checkerboard order (all “black” squares, then all “white”), and a flip that costs exactly nothing is accepted half the time, which keeps that order from dragging walls along. At $h=0$ the Wolff mode flips the colours back when the whole lattice changes sign, which is an exact symmetry, so the picture does not flash.

2Magnetisation: simulation, exact, mean field

Below $T_c$ the lattice picks a colour: the spontaneous magnetisation $m$ is not zero. Onsager (1944) and Yang (1952) found it exactly, $$m(T) = \big(1-\sinh(2/T)^{-4}\big)^{1/8}\quad (T < T_c),$$ so $m \sim (T_c-T)^{1/8}$ near the transition. The cheap mean-field guess, where every spin feels the average of its 4 neighbours, solves $m=\tanh(4m/T)$: right shape, wrong place ($T_c=4$), wrong exponent ($1/2$). The orange dot is your live simulator.

3The landscape: a bowl that splits

How likely is a lattice of magnetisation $m$? Roughly $e^{-N f(m)/T}$, where the free energy $f$ trades energy (agreement wants $|m|$ large) against entropy (there are many more ways to be mixed). In mean field, $$f(m) = \underbrace{-2m^2 - hm}_{\text{energy}} \;+\; T\underbrace{\Big[\tfrac{1+m}{2}\ln\tfrac{1+m}{2} + \tfrac{1-m}{2}\ln\tfrac{1-m}{2}\Big]}_{-\,\text{entropy}}.$$ Above $T=4$ it is a single bowl centred on $m=0$; below, the bottom pops up and two valleys appear at $\pm m^*$. This is the rate-function bowl of the Rare Events series, split in two by the interactions. (The vertical scale adapts to the curve; the dashed line is $f(0)$.)

5What light sees: critical opalescence

Light scattered by a material at an angle measures density (here: spin) fluctuations at one wavelength: the structure factor $$S(\mathbf k) = \tfrac1N\Big|\sum_{\mathbf r} (s_{\mathbf r}-\bar s)\, e^{-i\mathbf k\cdot\mathbf r}\Big|^2.$$ Long wavelengths sit at the centre of the right panel. Hot: fluctuations are small and short, a dim haze. Cold: almost nothing fluctuates, darkness. At $T_c$ fluctuations of every size appear, $S(k)\sim k^{-7/4}$, and the centre glows: this is why a fluid at its critical point turns milky.

“Glow” is the mean of $S$ over the few smallest wavevectors, averaged over time. Try sweeping $T$ through $T_c$ with Wolff clusters on: it peaks just above $T_c$ on a finite lattice.

6Memories as valleys: the Hopfield network

In 1982 John Hopfield read the Ising model backwards: choose the couplings $W_{ij}$ so that the patterns you want to remember are the low-energy states, $$E(s) = -\tfrac12\sum_{i\neq j} W_{ij}\, s_i s_j,$$ $$s_i \leftarrow \operatorname{sign}\Big(\sum_j W_{ij} s_j\Big).$$ Each update can only lower $E$, so a damaged picture rolls downhill into the nearest stored memory. Hebb's rule $W=\frac1N\sum_\mu \xi^\mu\xi^{\mu\top}$ handles a few distinct drawings but gets confused when they are similar or numerous (try storing 5); the pseudo-inverse rule removes their overlap first. Hopfield shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Physics with Geoffrey Hinton for this line of work.

7Going further

Kramers–Wannier duality: where $T_c$ comes from

Draw the domain walls of a configuration: they form closed loops on the dual lattice, and the weight of a loop configuration is $e^{-2\beta\,\ell}$ with $\ell$ the total length. Expanding $e^{\beta s_is_j}$ in powers of $\tanh\beta$ also produces closed loops, with weight $(\tanh\beta)^\ell$. So the low-temperature model at $\beta$ is the high-temperature model at $\beta^*$ with $$e^{-2\beta^*} = \tanh\beta \iff \sinh(2\beta)\sinh(2\beta^*) = 1.$$ A unique transition must sit at the self-dual point $\sinh(2/T_c) = 1$, i.e. $T_c = 2/\ln(1+\sqrt2)$ (1941, three years before Onsager confirmed it).

Peierls' argument: why 2D orders and 1D does not

In 1D a single domain wall costs $2J$ but can sit at any of $N$ places: entropy $\ln N$ wins, so there is no order at any $T>0$ (Ising's 1925 result). In 2D a wall around an island of perimeter $\ell$ costs $2J\ell$, and there are at most about $3^\ell$ such walls (a self-avoiding walk chooses between 3 directions per step). The wall free energy per unit length is $$2J - T\ln 3 > 0 \quad\text{for } T < \frac{2}{\ln 3}\approx 1.82,$$ so large islands are exponentially rare and the lattice stays ordered: a rigorous lower bound on $T_c \approx 2.27$.

Critical exponents and universality

Near $T_c$, $m\sim(T_c-T)^\beta$ and the correlation length $\xi\sim|T-T_c|^{-\nu}$. The exponents do not depend on the lattice or the material, only on the dimension and the symmetry: liquid–gas critical points and binary alloys share the 3D Ising values.

Cluster algorithms vs critical slowing down

Metropolis flips one spin at a time, so turning over a domain of size $\xi$ takes a time $\tau\sim\xi^{z}$ with $z\approx 2.17$: at $T_c$ on an $L\times L$ lattice, $\tau\sim L^{2.17}$. Swendsen and Wang (1987) and Wolff (1989) instead connect equal neighbours with probability $$p = 1 - e^{-2/T}$$ and flip whole clusters. These clusters are exactly the critical droplets, so $z$ drops to about $0.25$ (or less): the right move for the right scale. All the simulation points on this page were made that way.

Interfaces at $T_c$: SLE(3) curves of dimension 11/8

Fix the spins up on one half of the boundary of a domain and down on the other: a single interface runs between the two. At $T_c$, in the limit of a fine lattice, it converges to a random fractal curve, the Schramm–Loewner evolution SLE$_\kappa$ with $\kappa=3$, of Hausdorff dimension $$d = 1 + \frac{\kappa}{8} = \frac{11}{8}.$$ Stanislav Smirnov proved this conformal invariance (Fields Medal 2010), turning physicists' predictions into theorems.

8A short history

  1. 1920Wilhelm Lenz proposes the model to explain ferromagnetism.
  2. 1925His student Ernst Ising solves it in 1D: no transition. He wrongly guesses the same in every dimension.
  3. 1936Rudolf Peierls shows that in 2D the order survives at low temperature.
  4. 1941Hendrik Kramers and Gregory Wannier locate $T_c$ exactly with duality.
  5. 1944Lars Onsager solves the 2D model exactly: free energy, $T_c$, and a log-divergent specific heat.
  6. 1952Chen Ning Yang proves the formula for the spontaneous magnetisation, exponent 1/8.
  7. 1953Metropolis, the Rosenbluths and the Tellers invent the Monte Carlo rule used on this page.
  8. 1966–71Leo Kadanoff's block spins, then Kenneth Wilson's renormalisation group (Nobel Prize 1982).
  9. 1982John Hopfield turns the model into an associative memory (Nobel Prize 2024, with Geoffrey Hinton).
